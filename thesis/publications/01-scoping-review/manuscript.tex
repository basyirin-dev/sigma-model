\begin{abstract}
\textbf{Background:} AGI safety has grown into a large, fast-moving field, but existing reviews are fragmented and under-cover the preprint and forum literature where much of the field develops. Whether the compositional-generalisation and alignment literatures connect remains open.

\textbf{Objectives:} To map the English-language AGI safety literature (2015--2026, peer-reviewed and grey) into a subdomain taxonomy, methods catalogue, and treatment of internal representation; to locate where compositional generalisation and alignment research intersect; and to identify evidence gaps relevant to schema-coherence accounts.

\textbf{Methods:} A PRISMA-ScR scoping review across six databases plus grey literature was conducted. Of 5,191 records identified, 2,867 were screened; 1,268 records (1,136 unique studies) were included. Fields were charted by a hybrid pipeline (consistency ICC 0.930) and credibility scored on an eight-dimension rubric (kappa 0.924--1.000); open coding yielded 13 axial themes.

\textbf{Results:} The corpus grew sharply (72.6\% from 2024--2026). Value alignment dominated (89.8\%); mesa-optimization was thin (6.4\%). Only 24.7\% reached the high-credibility tiers; evaluation, the largest empirical strand, was among the least credible (251 studies; 17.9\%). 15.5\% discussed internal representations, mostly operationally. Under a reviewer-defined heuristic (not endorsement), 40.8\% carried a schema-coherence relevance signal; only 8 studies used both vocabularies.

\textbf{Conclusions:} This review provides a provisional credibility-stratified map of the field: the ingredients of a schema-coherence account are present, explicit connections between these literatures remain limited, and the evidence base is uneven: the largest empirical strand (evaluation) is the least credible, and the most credible strands are the least empirical. These findings provide a baseline for testing schema-coherence accounts of the generalisation--alignment relationship.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}
% ============================================================

Artificial general intelligence (AGI) safety has developed from a predominantly theoretical concern into a rapidly expanding research field spanning alignment, robustness, interpretability, evaluation, governance, and emergent failure modes~\cite{hendrycks2023overview,hendrycks2021unsolved,ji2023survey}. This expansion has produced substantial progress, but it has also generated a fragmented evidence base in which related problems are studied using different concepts, methods, and standards of evidence. In particular, research on alignment and research on generalisation have largely developed as distinct literatures, despite their shared concern with whether learned systems behave appropriately when conditions differ from those encountered during training~\cite{amodei2016concrete,geirhos2020shortcut,lake2018generalization,lake2023compositional,hupkes2023taxonomy,keysers2020measuring,liu2021towards,zhou2023domain,wang2022generalizing,karbasian2025unified,matta2024systematic}.

Existing reviews provide important partial maps of this landscape, but their coverage remains fragmented. Reviews of alignment, AI safety, and AGI risk have differed in scope, terminology, publication coverage, and methodological emphasis~\cite{triantafyllopoulos2026value,shen2024bidirectional,gyevnar2025aisafety,mclean2021risks,slattery2024ai,ahmed2024future}. Preprints, workshops, and specialist forums are particularly important in AGI safety yet remain less consistently represented than peer-reviewed literature~\cite{gyevnar2025aisafety}. Table~\ref{tab:prior-reviews} demonstrates the gap directly: none of the closest prior reviews integrates the three elements this review maps together---subdomains, methods and formalisms, and the treatment of internal representation.

This creates a practical and theoretical gap. Practically, researchers lack a single account of where the AGI-safety literature is concentrated, which areas are supported by stronger or weaker evidence, and which methodological approaches remain underdeveloped. Theoretically, it remains unclear how the literature on compositional generalisation relates to the literature on alignment and whether internal representation structure is treated as a central safety property or primarily as an explanatory mechanism.

AGI safety is also distinct from narrow AI safety, and the boundary matters for what is mapped. Narrow AI safety addresses the immediate harms of deployed, task-specific systems---algorithmic bias, privacy violations, and adversarial vulnerability. AGI safety addresses the structural and existential hazards of highly generalised, autonomous, and self-improving systems, including inner misalignment, corrigibility failure, and loss of control~\cite{hendrycks2023overview,russell2019human,bostrom2014superintelligence}. A system that must remain aligned across an unbounded distribution of novel tasks poses questions that do not arise for a single-purpose classifier~\cite{russell2019human}.

A scoping review is the appropriate design for this question, and the choice is deliberate. The question is a mapping question: it asks how a broad, emerging field is structured, which methods are used where, and where the gaps are, rather than asking whether a specific intervention works against a comparator. Scoping reviews are the established methodology for exactly this situation---broad or emerging topics with heterogeneous evidence, including grey literature, where the goal is to chart concepts and gaps rather than to synthesise a homogeneous outcome~\cite{arksey2005scoping,levac2010scoping,tricco2018prismascr}. A systematic review would be premature here because the field lacks a settled outcome measure (the same phenomenon is reported as accuracy gaps, benchmark failures, and governance analyses), and a narrative review would not provide the reproducible, protocol-driven map that a research programme needs as its evidence base. The scoping design therefore follows from the research question rather than being imposed on it.

The present scoping review addresses this gap by mapping English-language AGI-safety research published between 2015 and 2026 across peer-reviewed and grey-literature sources. It examines five questions concerning the field's subdomains, formal methods, treatment of internal representation, intersection between compositional generalisation and alignment, and evidence gaps relevant to schema-coherence accounts.

Importantly, the review does not test or establish the $\sigma$-trap hypothesis---the claim that compositional generalisation failure and alignment failure share a common low-schema-coherence mechanism~\cite{amsyar2026sigma}. Schema coherence is used solely as a heuristic lens to locate where relevant evidence appears and where connections are absent; the resulting map is an evidence baseline against which that hypothesis---and any competing account---can subsequently be evaluated.

The research question guiding this scoping review, specified prospectively in a registered protocol (https://osf.io/ntuh2/), asks how the landscape of AGI safety research is structured and where the gaps relevant to a schema-coherence account lie. To make the review's purpose unambiguous, we decompose this question into three component questions that jointly exhaust it:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{RQ1 (mapping):} how is the AGI safety research landscape structured across safety subdomains, methodological and formal approaches, and the treatment of internal representation?
\item \textbf{RQ2 (intersection):} where do compositional generalisation and alignment research intersect within that landscape?
\item \textbf{RQ3 (gaps):} what evidence gaps are relevant to the development and empirical evaluation of schema-coherence accounts of AGI safety?
\end{enumerate}
The first two questions are mapping questions; the third is explicitly theory-motivated. Five registered sub-questions operationalise these: (SQ1) what taxonomy of subdomains the literature exhibits and (SQ2) how formal methods and mathematical formalisms are distributed (RQ1); (SQ3) how internal representation structure is treated in safety research (RQ1); (SQ4) how the compositional generalisation and alignment literatures relate (RQ2); and (SQ5) what gaps exist that a schema-coherence framework could address (RQ3). The distinction matters for interpretation: RQ1 and RQ2 describe the literature as it exists, while RQ3 asks where the literature is silent. Five objectives map onto the sub-questions: to produce a subdomain taxonomy, a catalogue of methods, a structure-safety map, an intersection analysis, and a gap analysis.

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:background} defines the core concepts and traces the development of the field. Section~\ref{sec:methods} describes the scoping-review methodology, following the PRISMA extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR)~\cite{tricco2018prismascr} (see Appendix~\ref{app:prisma-scr} for the completed 22-item checklist). Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the results: a descriptive numerical summary, subdomain-by-subdomain findings, cross-cutting themes, and an analysis of gaps. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} interprets the findings, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Background}\label{sec:background}
% ============================================================

The vocabulary of AGI safety is unsettled, and the absence of a canonical taxonomy is itself a finding about the field. The core terms---alignment, safety, robustness, interpretability---are used with different meanings across the decision-theoretic tradition that founded the field, the machine-learning community that now dominates it empirically, and the governance literature that regulates it~\cite{bostrom2014superintelligence,russell2019human,hendrycks2023overview}. Value alignment, for example, denotes both a technical target (an objective function consistent with human preferences~\cite{amodei2016concrete}) and a contested normative project (whose values, elicited how, and aggregated under which conditions~\cite{triantafyllopoulos2026value}). This terminological pluralism is a genuine weakness: it limits comparability across studies, complicates synthesis, and lets superficially similar claims talk past one another~\cite{gyevnar2025aisafety,shen2024bidirectional}. It is also, however, the honest state of a field whose boundaries are still being drawn, and it motivates the first sub-question of this review (SQ1): rather than imposing a taxonomy, we chart the taxonomy the literature actually exhibits, and we define each term operationally where we use it.

The field has developed in identifiable waves, each with a distinct object of study, and each wave's framing carries assumptions that the next wave inherits or rejects. Early work in the 2000s was theoretical: it formalised goal structures such as Coherent Extrapolated Volition~\cite{yudkowsky2004cev} and the control problem, treating the agent as an abstract optimiser whose specification could be reasoned about in isolation~\cite{bostrom2014superintelligence,soares2015corrigibility}. The deep-learning era of the 2010s reframed safety around concrete, empirical failure modes---reward hacking, safe exploration, distributional shift---and, crucially, around the behaviour of trained systems rather than their internal structure~\cite{amodei2016concrete,hendrycks2021unsolved,hubinger2019risks}. This behavioural framing proved enormously productive, but it rests on an assumption that the third wave, from roughly 2021 onward, explicitly challenges: that the internal states of a model are either irrelevant to safety or unobservable. The internal-structure wave---eliciting latent knowledge~\cite{christiano2021elk}, natural abstractions~\cite{wentworth2020natural}, mechanistic interpretability~\cite{bereska2024mechanistic}, the study of deceptive alignment~\cite{hubinger2024sleeper}---treats the model's representations as the safety surface itself~\cite{huvard2023notion}. This progression from external behaviour to internal structure is the historical context in which schema coherence and the $\sigma$-trap arise as organising concepts, and it motivates SQ3: how, and how rigorously, does the current literature treat internal representation structure as a safety property~\cite{amsyar2026sigma}?

Schema coherence ($\sigma_A$) is the degree to which a model's internal representations reflect the latent generative structure of the data. The $\sigma$-trap is the failure regime in which training dynamics suppress schema coherence, producing models that fit the surface statistics of their training distribution and therefore fail to generalise compositionally or remain aligned under distribution shift~\cite{amsyar2026sigma}. The hypothesis underlying this review treats compositional generalisation failure and alignment failure as two faces of this single phenomenon. Two caveats govern the use of this lens here, and both follow from the critical stance taken throughout this review. First, the lens is novel: schema coherence has no established measurement instrument, and its closest relatives in the literature---goal misgeneralisation~\cite{langosco2022goal}, mesa-optimisation~\cite{hubinger2019risks}, and mechanistic-interpretability constructs such as features and circuits~\cite{bereska2024mechanistic}---are operationalised differently in different papers~\cite{amodei2016concrete,huvard2023notion}. Second, the review therefore uses the lens heuristically, as an organising device for asking where the ingredients of the hypothesis are present in the corpus and where the connecting joints are missing, without claiming that the hypothesis is established by the review itself. These caveats motivate SQ4 and SQ5: whether the compositional-generalisation and alignment literatures relate at all, and which gaps a schema-coherence framework would need to close~\cite{amsyar2026sigma,huvard2023notion}.

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}
% ============================================================

This scoping review was conducted and reported in accordance with the PRISMA extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR)~\cite{tricco2018prismascr,arksey2005scoping,levac2010scoping}, with a protocol registered prospectively on the Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/ntuh2/).

\subsection{Protocol and Registration}
The protocol---including the research question and sub-questions, objectives, eligibility criteria, search strategy, data-charting plan, quality-assessment plan, and synthesis plan---was specified in full before the search began and registered prospectively on the Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/ntuh2/), following the reporting requirements for scoping-review protocols~\cite{arksey2005scoping,levac2010scoping,shamseer2015prismap,tricco2018prismascr}. The review is reported against the 22-item PRISMA-ScR checklist~\cite{tricco2018prismascr} (Appendix~\ref{app:prisma-scr}); the completed checklist and the PRISMA flow diagram (Figure~\ref{fig:prisma-flow}) follow the corresponding reporting guidance~\cite{page2021prisma2020}. No protocol amendments were required.

\subsection{Eligibility Criteria}
Studies were eligible if they addressed AGI safety as defined in Section~\ref{sec:background}---that is, the structural, technical, or existential hazards of highly generalised systems---rather than narrow AI safety alone. The Population-Concept-Context (PCC) framework, the scoping-review analogue of PICO~\cite{arksey2005scoping,levac2010scoping}, was used to structure the criteria: the population is the AI/AGI research community; the concept is AGI safety frameworks, approaches, and failure modes; the context is peer-reviewed and grey literature published between 2015 and 2026. Inclusion required (i) a structural AGI-safety component, (ii) publication in 2015--2026, and (iii) English language. Records addressing only narrow AI safety concerns (algorithmic bias, privacy, post-hoc interpretability of narrow systems) without a structural AGI-safety component were excluded, as were records outside the date range or not in English.

Two restrictions merit explicit justification. The 2015 lower bound coincides with the empirical reframing of the field: the deep-learning-era formulation of concrete safety problems, which defines the modern research programme, was published in 2016~\cite{amodei2016concrete}, and the pre-2015 literature is largely theoretical and already covered by prior reviews of AGI risk~\cite{bostrom2014superintelligence,hendrycks2023overview}. Restricting to English follows the precedent of comparable reviews of this field and reflects practical access constraints; the grey-literature sources included (arXiv, PhilPapers, the AI Alignment Forum) are predominantly English-language~\cite{gyevnar2025aisafety}.

To make the eligibility decision reproducible rather than judgment-dependent, the ``structural AGI-safety component'' criterion was applied through an explicit operational rule: \emph{a record was classified as addressing AGI safety when its stated research object concerned the risks, control, alignment, generalisation, robustness, interpretability, evaluation, or governance of highly generalised, autonomous, or potentially self-improving systems, rather than harms specific to a fixed narrow task}. Three borderline cases illustrate how the rule was applied. A study of algorithmic bias in a deployed credit-scoring model was excluded under R-STRUCT: the harms it addresses are specific to a fixed narrow task, with no structural AGI-safety component, even though it concerns AI harm. A study of reward hacking in reinforcement learning agents was included: although it may not frame itself in AGI terms, its research object (a learned agent optimising a misspecified proxy objective) falls under the structural failure-mode clause. A formal analysis of transformer expressivity with no safety engagement was excluded under R-SUBJ: it concerns generalisation performance but does not address an AGI-safety subdomain. The exclusion categories mirror these distinctions---R-STRUCT (858 records), R-SUBJ (547), R-DATE (141), R-LANG (43). The precision of the uncertain tail was checked by independent adjudication: of the 15 records the original screen flagged uncertain and carried to full text (all 15 ultimately included), the author's independent re-screen agreed on 12 (80\%) and would have excluded 3 HCI-flavoured value-alignment applications (a multimodal chatbot, an experiential-value framework, and a helpline-counsellor training study); Cohen's kappa is not reported for this subset because the final-status column is constant.

\subsection{Information Sources and Search Strategy}
The search was built as a block-structured intersection query around three concept blocks---safety, generalisation, and internal structure---because pilot searches confirmed that flat union queries return tens of thousands of records while the intersection of safety and structural-generalisation terms is where the schema-coherence-relevant literature actually lives~\cite{hendrycks2023overview,sinha2024systematic}. The search distinguished two roles. \textbf{F1, the discovery search}, established the broad corpus with a single safety-and-alignment query; \textbf{F2--F4, targeted supplementary searches}, ensured coverage of specific conceptual clusters (generalisation, internal structure, and schema coherence) whose vocabulary is sparse in standard databases and whose records would otherwise be missed. The targeted families are supplementary by design: they exist to confirm that the discovery search did not miss a relevant cluster, not to define the corpus. Four query families were defined (F1 broad safety; F2 safety $\cap$ generalisation; F3 safety $\cap$ structure $\cap$ safety qualifiers; F4 schema-coherence exploratory) and adapted to the syntax of each database. Terminology was not constructed from the review's own construct vocabulary alone: for each major concept (AGI safety, alignment, generalisation, internal representation, interpretability), the closest established review was used as a nearest-neighbour source, and its search terminology was inspected and adapted (e.g., alignment-survey and generalisation-survey terminology~\cite{ji2023survey,sinha2024systematic,liu2021towards}). We searched six databases: Scopus, Web of Science, ACM Digital Library, IEEE Xplore, arXiv, and PhilPapers, executed in July--August 2026 with coverage 2015--2026; the full database-specific strings are in Appendix~\ref{app:search-strings}. The database search was supplemented with grey literature and citation tracking: Google Scholar for grey sources, and OpenAlex and Semantic Scholar for forward/backward citation chaining from the protocol's seed papers. The AI Alignment Forum corpus was included manually---forum posts on safety-relevant topics identified through the curated phase-0.5 review library during protocol development, rather than a platform-wide crawl. Forum posts were treated as records on the same footing as other sources; duplicates between forum posts and arXiv preprints of the same work were resolved by the version-pair collapse described in Section~\ref{sec:methods}. Venue and terminology planning drew on the phase-0.5 venue analysis, which documented the field's ~50\% preprint rate and its dependence on workshop and grey sources~\cite{gyevnar2025aisafety}.

\subsubsection{Search sensitivity}
Because the targeted supplementary families are theory-oriented, we assessed whether the central findings depend on them. The broad discovery search alone identified 1,110 of the 1,136 unique studies (97.7\%); the targeted families contributed 26 (2.3\%). Excluding all non-discovery-sourced studies leaves the headline distributions essentially unchanged: value alignment 89.8\% $\rightarrow$ 90.2\%, mesa-optimization 6.4\% $\rightarrow$ 5.9\%, internal-representation coverage 15.5\% $\rightarrow$ 15.3\%, and compositional-generalisation vocabulary 44 $\rightarrow$ 40 studies. The central findings therefore do not depend on the theory-oriented search families; the full comparison is provided as supplementary material.

\subsection{Screening}
Records were deduplicated programmatically (exact-title and DOI matching), removing 2,324 duplicate records from the 5,191 identified. Screening proceeded in three stages: title screening, abstract screening, and full-text assessment, performed by the single author using Rayyan~\cite{ouzzani2016rayyan} with a calibration set used to fix inclusion thresholds before the main pass. The single-reviewer design was a deliberate resource-constrained choice to maximise field coverage (1,136 included studies from 1,268 included records across six databases and grey literature). Its reproducibility rests on four compensating features: pre-specified eligibility criteria with an explicit operational rule (Section~\ref{sec:methods}), calibration against a shared threshold set, a full audit trail of screening decisions, and consistency sampling at the extraction stage. As a screening-reliability check, a random 15\% sample (430 records) of the 2,867 screened records was re-screened by applying the operational eligibility rule mechanically to title and abstract: raw agreement with the original calibrated decisions was 69\% (Cohen's kappa 0.39), with 72\% agreement at the abstract stage where the re-screen and the original screen used comparable information. As a human-adjudicated check, the author independently re-screened a random 100-record sample (50 included, 50 excluded; original decisions hidden): raw agreement with the original decisions was 74\% (Cohen's kappa 0.48, precision/recall on inclusion 74\%/74\%), exceeding the mechanical rule's agreement. The disagreement pattern is systematic and informative: the author would exclude 13 human-AI interaction studies that were included, and would include 13 safety-adjacent conceptual studies (mechanistic interpretability, corrigibility, bootstrapped alignment) that were excluded, indicating that the original screen's boundary was liberal toward human-interaction work relative to the author's own current application of the rule. The residual disagreement is the expected gap between mechanical rule application and calibrated judgment, and it is precisely why the original screening used calibration; the checks are reported transparently and do not substitute for independent dual-reviewer screening, which remains the review's primary limitation (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}). Figure~\ref{fig:prisma-flow} summarises the flow of records. A total of 5,191 records were identified from databases and supplementary sources; after removing 2,324 duplicates, 2,867 records were screened at title and abstract level, and 1,589 were excluded. The remaining 1,278 records proceeded to full-text assessment, of which 10 were excluded (3 for not addressing AGI-safety structure, 7 for no AGI-safety subdomain engagement), leaving 1,268 included records, representing 1,136 unique studies after collapsing duplicate versions of the same study (typically an arXiv preprint and its published counterpart; the reconciliation log is in the supplementary material). The screening was deliberately liberal---records were only excluded at title/abstract when clearly outside scope (R-STRUCT, R-SUBJ, R-DATE, R-LANG), with uncertain records carried forward to full-text assessment---which is why 1,278 records (44.6\% of those screened) proceeded to full text and only 10 were excluded there. Exclusion reasons at screening were recorded in the four categories---not structural AGI safety (858), no AGI-safety subdomain engagement (547), outside the 2015--2026 date range (141), and not in English (43)---mirroring the eligibility criteria.

\subsection{Data Extraction}
A 25-field charting schema was developed, piloted on five studies spanning the subdomain and publication-type space, and finalised before the full run (available in the supplementary materials). The fields fall into six groups: bibliographic (paper identifier, title, authors, year, venue, DOI, citation count); classification (publication type, subdomains, methodology); formal content (formal framework, mathematical formalism, key equations and definitions); structure-relevance (whether the study discusses internal representations, whether it discusses schema coherence, its sigma-trap relevance rating, and the justification for that rating); empirical detail (datasets used, sample size, effect sizes); and reporting (key contribution, limitations stated, open questions, evidence basis, notes). Structured fields were charted by a hybrid pipeline: scripted heuristic extraction from full texts, followed by a large-language-model (LLM)-assisted extraction pass with audit-trail merging of every revision, mirroring the dual-extraction discipline of systematic reviews~\cite{whiting2011quadas2}. A 20\% random sample (254 studies) was independently re-extracted by a second implementation to estimate inter-extractor agreement: Cohen's kappa for categorical fields and an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.930 on the continuous sigma-relevance rating.

\subsubsection{LLM-assisted pipeline disclosure}
The charting and credibility passes used an external instruction-tuned LLM assistance pipeline. The full prompt batches are archived (supplementary material, `research/charting/ai-prompt-batches/`); the workflow was: scripted heuristic prefill, then an LLM-assisted pass whose every revision was merged with an audit trail, then scripted consistency checks. Three transparency points are stated plainly. First, the specific model and version were not fixed across batches---they varied with the provider's deployment over the July--August 2026 window---so the prompts, not the model, are the reproducible artefact. Second, the 20\% consistency sample was re-extracted by an independent scripted implementation, not by a human gold standard; the reported ICC and kappa therefore measure pipeline-internal consistency, not accuracy against independent expert judgement. Third, to anchor at least one statistic to a human-adjudicated baseline, a spot-check of charted records was independently verified against their full texts; the results are reported in Section~\ref{sec:methods}.

\subsubsection{Human-adjudicated spot-check}
A random sample of 30 charted records with locally available full texts was independently verified against those texts by the author, checking four high-inference fields: subdomains, methodology, formal framework, and the sigma-trap relevance rating. Agreement with the source documents was 24/30 (80\%) for subdomains, 28/30 (93\%) for methodology, 25/30 (83\%) for formal framework, and 26/30 (87\%) for the relevance rating; the record-by-record comparison is in the supplementary material. As a further human anchor on the highest-inference variable, the author independently re-rated $\sigma$-trap relevance on a separate 30-study sample (15 signal, 15 non-signal; original ratings hidden): exact-scale agreement was 80\% (Cohen's kappa 0.72, all six disagreements within one point, no directional bias), and agreement on signal membership (rating $\geq$ 4) was 100\% (kappa 1.00). These are lenient keyword-based consistency checks against the source texts plus the author-adjudicated relevance check, which together provide the non-pipeline-anchored reliability statistics in the review, alongside the ICC/kappa consistency checks of the automated passes.

\subsection{Evidence-Credibility Assessment}
Evidence credibility was assessed as a signal, not an exclusion criterion: no study was removed from the review on credibility grounds, consistent with scoping-review practice and PRISMA-ScR item 12~\cite{tricco2018prismascr}. Two clarifications are required to avoid over-reading the assessment. First, this is not a conventional risk-of-bias assessment: it does not judge the internal validity of each study's causal claims, and it must not be read as such. Second, the dimensions are credibility indicators, not methodological-quality scores: they combine source characteristics (venue tier, author authority, citation uptake) with methodological signals (formal-methods rigour, empirical reproducibility, argumentative rigour, transparency, prior-literature engagement). The source-based dimensions merit explicit defence. Author authority is included because, in a field with a large anonymous and first-time literature, prior contribution is informative about the reliability of a claim's provenance; citation uptake is included as a time-normalised measure of field engagement. Both are reported transparently and separately in the rubric so that readers can re-weight them or drop them (Appendix~\ref{app:quality}). Each study was scored on the eight weighted dimensions (D1--D8) using a paper-type-adjusted rubric adapted from QUADAS-2, ROBINS-I, and PROBAST+AI~\cite{whiting2011quadas2,sterne2016robinsi,moons2025probastaialpha}. Scripted rules scored the venue- and citation-based dimensions; an LLM-assisted rating pass scored the remaining dimensions, with a 20\% independent-rater consistency sample yielding per-dimension kappa values of 0.924--1.000 and a composite ICC of 0.947. These statistics establish consistency of the operationalisation under the consistency protocol; they do not establish that the underlying coding constructs are valid, nor that LLM-assisted coding is equivalent to independent expert review. Composite scores were mapped to tiers A--E (A = 2, B = 279, C = 735, D = 118, E = 2), where A--B denote high credibility, C moderate, and D--E low. Because credibility is a signal rather than a filter, a sensitivity analysis compared conclusions on the full set against the high-credibility subset (tiers A+B, n = 281): the prevalence of the heuristic schema-coherence signal was 40.8\% in the full set and 49.5\% in the subset, indicating that the headline conclusions are robust to credibility filtering. The rubric and full per-study scores are provided as supplementary material and summarised in Appendix~\ref{app:quality}.

\subsection{Synthesis}
The synthesis was descriptive and thematic, consistent with the guidance for synthesis without meta-analysis in scoping reviews~\cite{campbell2020swim,harrison2017albatross} and with GRADE-informed reporting of confidence in the evidence~\cite{guyatt2008grade}. The descriptive strand summarised the corpus by publication type, subdomain, year, venue, methodology, and formal content, addressing SQ1 and SQ2. The thematic strand proceeded by open coding of the charted text fields, grouping open codes into 13 axial themes, and mapping themes to the schema-coherence constructs of the review, addressing SQ3--SQ5. Theme membership was operationalised as keyword rules over the charted fields so that the synthesis is reproducible; theme sizes are indicative, and the full charted data, theme statistics, and rules are provided as supplementary material. Where quantitative claims about credibility or prevalence are made, they are reported with the quality assessment as context rather than as weighted evidence, reflecting the credibility-signal design of the quality assessment.

\subsection{Data Quality and Missing Data}
The charting pipeline included explicit quality checks. The key-contribution field was completed for 941 of 1,136 studies (82.8\%); the remainder were charted on a metadata basis and contribute to the descriptive statistics but not to the thematic coding, a limitation reported in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}. Controlled-vocabulary fields (subdomains, methodology, formal framework) passed automated consistency checks with zero violations after normalisation, and numerical fields (year, relevance, citation count) passed range checks. Missing data were handled by field: the 10 studies without a year are excluded only from time-series analyses; the 197 metadata-basis studies are retained for coverage and flagged throughout the synthesis. The 20\% consistency sample (254 studies) bounds pipeline-internal charting reliability (ICC 0.930 on the continuous relevance rating), and the full data-quality report is provided as supplementary material.

\subsection{Descriptive Overview}
The results are organised around five stories. First, what the field is: its subdomain structure, volume, and growth. Second, what methods it uses: the methodology and formalism gradient across the corpus. Third, how credible its evidence is: the A--E distribution and its correlates. Fourth, whether the field studies internal structure: the internal-representation findings. Fifth, where the bridge is: the compositional-generalisation--alignment intersection and the schema-coherence gaps. Each story corresponds to a component question (RQ1--RQ3) and to the registered sub-questions (SQ1--SQ5), as mapped in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

The review included 1,136 unique studies (from 1,268 included records; 132 duplicate versions of the same study---typically an arXiv preprint and its published counterpart---were collapsed to one entry each; the reconciliation log is in the supplementary material). The corpus grew sharply over time: annual counts rose from 1 in 2015 to 344 in 2025 (275 in 2026). 2026 is a partial year---searches were executed in July--August 2026---so the 2026 count is not comparable to full years and the decline from 344 (2025) to 275 (2026) reflects the truncated window, not a field contraction. 825 studies (72.6\%) were published in 2024--2026 (825 of the 1,126 year-known studies, 73.3\%); 10 studies had no recorded year. \subsection{Results by Subdomain}
Table~\ref{tab:subdomains} reports the volume, growth, empirical share, and credibility of each subdomain; the following highlights what matters for interpretation. \textbf{Value alignment} (1,020; 89.8\%) is the ubiquitous framing, co-occurring with every named subdomain in at least 92\% of cases; its literature is dominated by proxy-reward failures (e.g., the formal characterisation of reward hacking~\cite{P688}), preference and value learning, and ethics, at baseline credibility (25.6\% tier A+B). \textbf{Ethics} (603) is the second-largest subdomain, above baseline credibility (30.0\%) but among the least empirical (34.5\%). \textbf{Robustness} (537) couples strongly with the reward-hacking theme (Jaccard 0.205), treating proxy misspecification as a robustness problem. \textbf{Capabilities and control} (406) has the highest credibility among the large subdomains (28.6\% tier A+B, including both tier-A studies in the corpus). \textbf{Interpretability} (360) has the lowest theme-level credibility of any theme (13.8\% tier A+B), a large but weakly validated measurement literature. \textbf{Governance} (291) is the least empirical subdomain (33.7\%) yet the highest-credibility one (33.3\% tier A+B), including principal-agent~\cite{P007} and incentive-compatibility~\cite{P128} framings of misalignment. \textbf{Mesa-optimization} (73) is the smallest $\sigma$-trap-critical subdomain, carrying high-credibility deceptive-alignment work (41.4\% tier A+B, e.g., monitoring proposals~\cite{P1097}) while remaining thinly covered relative to its role in alignment failure.

\subsection{Cross-Cutting Themes}
Thematic analysis grouped open codes into 13 axial themes (Table~\ref{tab:themes}). All themes spanned at least three subdomains; their couplings define four clusters.

The \emph{reward-hacking cluster} linked proxy-reward and reward-hacking work (178 studies), preference and value learning (123), and robustness (140): the strongest couplings in the corpus (Jaccard 0.214 and 0.205). Proxy misspecification was the fastest-growing theme, from 3 studies in 2023 to 70 in 2025, and it is the connective tissue between alignment, robustness, and capabilities in the empirical literature.

The \emph{schema-coherence cluster} linked internal representation structure and schema (88 studies) with compositional generalization and the $\sigma$-trap (158; coupling 0.183). Representation structure was discussed operationally---features, activations, latents---rather than axiomatically: 176 studies (15.5\%) discussed internal representations, but only 14 used the phrase ``internal representation'' literally, and the theme sits slightly above baseline credibility (25.0\% and 32.9\% tier A+B respectively).

The \emph{mechanism cluster} coupled interpretability and mechanistic analysis (65 studies) with the schema-coherence cluster (0.133): mechanistic work is where representation structure is actually examined. The \emph{society cluster} coupled governance (110) with capabilities and control (183; 0.149) and ethics (296): the risk framing runs from technical control into sociotechnical governance.

Two descriptive patterns follow from the theme structure. First, credibility is not uniform across themes: the theoretically framed strands (formal methods 42.2\%, capabilities and control 41.5\%, governance 39.1\%, deceptive alignment 41.4\%) carry roughly twice the high-credibility share of the empirical strands (proxy reward 18.5\%, evaluation 17.9\%, interpretability 13.8\%). Second, the schema-coherence-related themes (internal representation structure, compositional generalization and the $\sigma$-trap) sit between these poles (25.0\% and 32.9\%).

\subsection{Gaps}
The charted data confirmed all five gaps pre-specified in the protocol's gap analysis and surfaced three new ones that were not pre-specified---these new gaps (evaluation infrastructure quality, governance empirics, deceptive-alignment detection) are the independently discovered findings of this review, as distinct from the confirmatory component. (i) \emph{Internal representation structure as a safety property} (G1): 176 studies discussed internal representations, of which 96 carried a formal framework, but the vocabulary of structure---``schema'' in 40 studies, ``compositionality'' in 0---was not axiomatised as a safety property. (ii) \emph{Compositional-generalisation-to-alignment bridge} (G2): ``compositional generalization'' appeared in 44 studies (listed in Supplementary Table S4), ``goal misgeneralization'' in none (verified against the search terms in Appendix~\ref{app:search-strings} across all charted text fields---key contribution, relevance justification, and open questions---and the full-text basis where available), and the intersection with $\sigma$-trap language was 8 studies (Supplementary Table S5), characterised in Section~\ref{sec:results}. The absence is robust to synonym variants: ``systematic generalization'' appears in 1 study, ``generalisation failure'' in none, and the broader ``distribution shift'' in 7 (full synonym-sensitivity test in the supplementary material), so the finding reflects vocabulary sparsity rather than a narrow keyword rule. (iii) \emph{Dynamical-systems safety treatment} (G3): 18 studies (1.6\%) used a dynamical-systems framework. (iv) \emph{Schema theory} (G4): 40 studies (3.5\%) discussed a concept related to schema coherence; 29 used the phrase ``schema coherence'' literally. (v) \emph{Secondary gaps} (G5): continual-learning/forgetting vocabulary appeared in 2 studies and ``simplicity prior'' in none. Three new gaps emerged from the charted data: evaluation infrastructure quality (the evaluation theme, 251 studies, had 17.9\% tier A+B and included critiques of static-benchmark leakage~\cite{P864}), governance empirics (33.7\% empirical), and deceptive-alignment detection (29 studies, 2.6\% of the corpus despite 41.4\% tier A+B). Together these confirm the gap structure identified in the pre-registered protocol: 176 studies discuss internal representations, 44 use compositional-generalisation vocabulary, 8 of these also use $\sigma$-trap language, and the bridging term ``goal misgeneralization'' is absent from the charted records. A supplementary table reports each gap as pre-specified versus as confirmed by the charted data, so the confirmatory-by-design component (G1--G5) and the independently discovered component (the three new gaps) are transparently separable.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
% ============================================================

This scoping review mapped the landscape of AGI safety research as represented by 1,136 unique studies (from 1,268 included records) published between 2015 and 2026. The results describe a field that is large, recent, and skewed: 72.6\% of the corpus appeared in 2024--2026; value alignment is the near-universal framing (89.8\% of studies), while mesa-optimization, the subdomain closest to the $\sigma$-trap's structural failure mode, accounts for 6.4\%. The literature is dominated by empirical work (40.4\% of studies by type) that reports point estimates without uncertainty quantification, and the credibility assessment---used as a signal, not a filter---found that only 24.7\% of studies reach the high-credibility tiers.

A consequential finding concerns the structural relationships between the field's parts. Across the corpus, 176 studies (15.5\%) discuss internal representations, 464 (40.8\%) carry the heuristic schema-coherence relevance signal (a researcher-defined mapping variable, not evidence the studies endorse the construct), and 44 studies use compositional-generalisation vocabulary; the connecting vocabulary is nearly absent: ``goal misgeneralization'' appears in no charted record, ``compositionality'' in none, and the intersection between compositional-generalization and schema-coherence signal language is 8 studies. The compositional-generalisation and alignment literatures show limited explicit intersection in the searched corpus. This is consistent with the gap structure (G1--G5) and with the framing of the $\sigma$-trap as a hypothesis whose connecting evidence remains underdeveloped rather than as an established phenomenon~\cite{amsyar2026sigma}.

The review's findings align with, and extend, the existing review literature, but each prior review leaves a specific residue that this one addresses. Triantafyllopoulos et al.~\cite{triantafyllopoulos2026value} reviewed value alignment in advisory AI (83 studies) and concluded that fairness and cognition are underdeveloped and that evaluation is non-standardised; our corpus confirms both---the evaluation theme is the least credible large strand (17.9\% tier A+B) and the human-AI interaction theme (106 studies) is small relative to its role---but we additionally locate these weaknesses within a full subdomain map rather than within a single application domain. Shen et al.~\cite{shen2024bidirectional} argued that alignment is treated too statically and unidirectionally (400+ studies); our co-occurrence and theme data corroborate the narrowness (value alignment co-occurs with every other subdomain at $\geq$ 92\%, making it the default framing), while the governance theme's credibility premium (39.1\%) suggests the sociotechnical dimension they call for is emerging but empirically thin. Gyevnar and Kasirzadeh~\cite{gyevnar2025aisafety} explicitly flagged preprint and forum under-coverage; our inclusion of the grey-literature half of the corpus (arXiv alone 15.5\%, plus forum and workshop sources) is the direct response, and our data quantify how concentrated that grey layer is in global preprint platforms (supplementary venue-country analysis). The earlier AGI-risk reviews~\cite{hendrycks2023overview} predate the empirical wave that dominates our 2024--2026 strata, so they cannot speak to the credibility distribution we report. Across all four comparisons, the consistent picture is that the field's fragmentation is not merely topical but structural---subdomains, methods, and credibility are unevenly distributed in ways that single-domain reviews cannot see.

\subsection{Answers to the Review Questions}
Each component question receives an explicit answer. \textbf{RQ1 (mapping)}: the landscape is structured as a value-alignment-centred corpus---an eight-subdomain taxonomy dominated by value alignment (89.8\%), with mesa-optimization and governance as the thin tails (6.4\% and 25.6\%); 63.2\% of studies are formal under a permissive definition, concentrated in optimization, probability, and logic with dynamical-systems formalism nearly absent (1.6\%); and 15.5\% discuss internal representations, mostly operationally rather than axiomatically (SQ1--SQ3). \textbf{RQ2 (intersection)}: the compositional-generalisation and alignment literatures show limited explicit intersection---8 studies share both vocabularies and ``goal misgeneralization'' appears in none (SQ4). This is a vocabulary-level absence in the searched corpus: it does not establish that the underlying phenomena are conceptually disconnected, which would require semantic- or citation-level analysis beyond keyword mapping; the finding is reported as a vocabulary observation with that caveat. \textbf{RQ3 (gaps)}: five pre-specified gaps are confirmed and three new ones identified, all traceable to charted data (SQ5). Each answer is bounded by the review's credibility distribution: the strongest answers (RQ1) rest on the full corpus, while the structural answers (RQ2, RQ3) rest on a minority of studies whose credibility is, at best, moderate.

Three findings carry implications for research practice. First, evaluation infrastructure is the largest low-credibility strand: the evaluation theme (251 studies) has the second-largest membership but only 17.9\% high-credibility tiers, and the corpus itself contains critiques of static-benchmark leakage~\cite{P864}. The field's measurement base needs methodological investment. Second, governance---the highest-credibility subdomain (33.3\%)---is the least empirical (33.7\%), meaning its claims rest on conceptual analysis; sociotechnical and principal-agent framings~\cite{P007,P128} would benefit from empirical evaluation. Third, deceptive-alignment detection is small (29 studies) despite high credibility, and mesa-optimization remains thinly covered (6.4\%) relative to its role in alignment failure.

Read together, these patterns describe an inversion in the field's evidence structure: the empirical engine---evaluation and benchmarking work---is simultaneously the largest and the least trusted component of the corpus, while the most trusted component (governance and formal theory) is the least empirical. The inversion is a property of the corpus under the credibility rubric used here, and it is rubric-dependent: the index weights source characteristics (venue tier, author authority, citation uptake) alongside methodological signals, and the pattern shifts if those dimensions are excluded (sensitivity analyses in Appendix~\ref{app:quality} re-weight the index without author authority and citation uptake). It therefore describes the field's source-and-rigour structure, not an independent measure of methodological quality, and it shapes what the field can currently claim: its most-cited claims rest on the lowest scores on the source-and-rigour composite used here, and its highest-composite claims are its least-tested ones. This asymmetry, rather than any single finding, is the review's most consequential structural observation.

These results bound the claims that can be drawn from this corpus. They do not establish the $\sigma$-trap hypothesis; they establish that its ingredients are present in the literature, that explicit connections between the relevant literatures remain limited, and that the field's evidence base is uneven. For the broader field, the map's value is comparative: it provides a credibility-stratified baseline against which future reviews can measure the field's evolution, and it identifies where the field's self-correction mechanisms are weakest---evaluation infrastructure, governance empirics, and structural measurement.

\subsection{Limitations}
The limitations separate into those of the review process and those of the underlying evidence base; both bound the strength of the claims above.

\subsubsection{Review-process limitations}
First, the review was conducted by a single reviewer, including screening, charting, and thematic coding; dual-reviewer screening and dual-coder thematic analysis were not performed, and the thematic synthesis relies on keyword-rule operationalizations whose sizes are indicative rather than exact. As a partial mitigation, the author independently re-adjudicated three seeded samples with the original decisions hidden: a 100-record screening sample (74\% raw agreement, Cohen's kappa 0.48), a 30-study $\sigma$-trap relevance sample (80\% exact-scale agreement, kappa 0.72; 100\% agreement on signal membership, kappa 1.00), and the 15-record uncertain-screening tail (80\% agreement with final status). These provide a human anchor on the pipeline's highest-inference decisions but do not constitute independent dual-reviewer screening, which remains the review's primary limitation. Second, the field moves quickly: the corpus is a snapshot (searches run July--August 2026) in a literature whose volume doubled in two years, and the 2026 strata are partial-year counts. Third, grey literature is included by design (arXiv alone is 15.5\% of the corpus), which broadens coverage but introduces coverage bias toward sources indexed by the included platforms---forum posts outside the AI Alignment Forum and preprint servers outside arXiv are under-represented. Fourth, the supplementary venue-country provenance analysis uses a publisher/association-headquarters proxy covering 48.4\% of studies and does not attribute authors' institutions, so its findings are indicative of publication provenance, not research capacity. Fifth, the LLM-assisted extraction and credibility-scoring passes introduce tool-specific variability; the 20\% consistency samples (extraction ICC 0.930; credibility kappa 0.924--1.000, composite ICC 0.947) bound this variability but do not eliminate it, and the statistics establish consistency of the operationalisation rather than construct validity. Sixth, the thematic coding is a single-coder interpretation operationalised as keyword rules: it is reproducible but reflects one analytical stance, and theme boundaries are conventional.

\subsubsection{Evidence-base limitations}
The evidence base itself constrains the findings independently of the review process. First, 197 studies (17.3\%) were charted on a metadata basis and contribute little free text to the thematic analysis. Second, only 24.7\% of studies reach the high-credibility tiers, and several literatures the review discusses are dominated by lower-credibility work (evaluation 17.9\%, interpretability 13.8\% tier A+B). Third, governance---the highest-credibility subdomain---rests on 33.7\% empirical methods, so its claims are largely conceptual. Fourth, structural measurement is weak across the corpus: internal representation is discussed by 15.5\% of studies but rarely formalised as a safety property, and no validated schema-coherence measure exists. Fifth, the compositional-generalisation and alignment literatures show limited explicit intersection in the searched corpus; the review can report this absence but cannot distinguish a genuine conceptual disconnect from a vocabulary or indexing artefact. These evidence-base limitations are findings of the review as much as constraints on it.

\subsection{Implications for Structural Accounts of AGI Safety}
This scoping review provides a foundational evidence map that establishes a credibility-stratified baseline for the broader AGI safety research programme. By mapping 1,136 studies (from 1,268 included records) across subdomains, methodologies, and internal representation structures, the review identifies critical gaps---most notably, the limited explicit intersection between compositional generalisation and alignment literatures. This mapping function is essential because subsequent conceptual frameworks, such as schema-coherence and the $\sigma$-trap hypothesis, require an empirically grounded baseline against which to test their assumptions rather than relying on assertion. Ultimately, the map stands as an independent deliverable: its structural findings regarding the field's uneven evidence base and missing conceptual joints are valuable to the broader community, independent of any future theoretical frameworks.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
% ============================================================

This scoping review provides an integrated map of the AGI-safety literature across subdomains, methodological approaches, internal representation, and evidence credibility. Five findings are particularly important. First, the literature is strongly concentrated around value alignment, which co-occurs broadly with other safety subdomains. Second, formal approaches are unevenly distributed, with optimization, probability, and logic substantially more represented than dynamical-systems approaches. Third, internal representation is discussed by a substantial minority of studies, but it is rarely formalised as an explicit safety property. Fourth, the literature identified through our search shows only limited explicit intersection between compositional generalisation and alignment research. Fifth, the evidence base is uneven: large evaluation literatures coexist with comparatively limited representation of high-credibility evidence under the rubric used in this review.

These findings should be interpreted as a map of the literature rather than as evidence establishing the $\sigma$-trap or schema-coherence hypothesis. The schema-coherence lens was used heuristically to identify where relevant conceptual and empirical ingredients appear and where connections remain underdeveloped. In particular, the limited observed intersection between compositional-generalisation and alignment vocabularies should not be interpreted as proof that the underlying phenomena are unrelated; it indicates that the relationship is not yet well developed or explicitly investigated within the literature captured by the present search strategy.

The principal contribution of the review is therefore the identification of an evidence and research structure within which competing explanations can be tested. Future work should prioritise empirical measures of internal representation, direct tests linking generalisation behaviour to alignment-relevant outcomes, and evaluation designs that move beyond static benchmark performance. Because this scoping review relied on vocabulary and keyword mapping to identify structural intersections, future systematic reviews must employ deeper full-text semantic analysis to determine whether the apparent disconnect between compositional generalisation and alignment is a true conceptual silo, or merely a failure of shared terminology. Such work would provide a stronger basis for determining whether schema coherence represents a useful explanatory construct for AGI safety or whether the apparent connection reflects distinct phenomena that have developed in parallel.

The conclusions are necessarily bounded by the review methodology. Single-reviewer screening and thematic coding, grey-literature coverage, LLM-assisted extraction and credibility scoring, and the rapidly changing 2026 literature all limit the strength of causal or definitive claims. This review provides a reproducible, credibility-stratified map of the AGI-safety literature and a baseline against which the field's evolution can be measured. It identifies where the evidence is strong, where it is thin, and where the connecting concepts between subdomains remain underdeveloped. These findings are offered as a resource for the research community, independent of any particular theoretical programme.

\section{Full Search Strings}\label{app:search-strings}
% ============================================================

The search was block-structured around three concept blocks (safety, generalisation, internal structure). \textbf{F1} is the discovery search establishing the broad corpus; \textbf{F2--F4} are targeted supplementary searches ensuring coverage of specific conceptual clusters (the theory-oriented families, whose contribution is assessed in Section~\ref{sec:methods}). The exemplar Scopus string (PRISMA-ScR item 8) was:

\begin{verbatim}
F2 (safety block) AND (generalisation block):
TITLE-ABS-KEY ( "AI alignment" OR "AGI safety" OR "mesa-optimization"
  OR "deceptive alignment" OR "inner alignment" OR "corrigibility"
  OR "reward hacking" OR "value alignment" )
AND
TITLE-ABS-KEY ( "compositional generalization" OR "compositional generalisation"
  OR "systematic generalization" OR "compositional learning"
  OR "out-of-distribution generalization" OR "distribution shift" )

F3 (narrow intersection): safety block AND ("compositional generalization"
  OR "compositional generalisation" OR "systematic generalization"
  OR "compositional learning" OR "schema" OR "schematic"
  OR "representational structure" OR "latent structure"
  OR "internal representation" ) AND ( "safety" OR "alignment"
  OR "robustness" OR "interpretability" )
\end{verbatim}

The complete set of 14 strings (four query families $\times$ six databases, with syntax adaptations per database) is provided in the protocol appendix and as supplementary material (`research/search-terms.md`).

\section{Quality Assessment Rubric and Scores}\label{app:quality}

The rubric described in Section~\ref{sec:methods} is a \emph{source-and-rigour index}: ``credibility'' throughout this review is shorthand for that index, which combines source characteristics (venue tier, author authority, citation uptake) with methodological signals (formal-methods rigour, empirical reproducibility, argumentative rigour, transparency, prior-literature engagement). It is not a risk-of-bias or general methodological-quality assessment.

Because the source-based dimensions may disadvantage newer, grey, or less-established literature, a sensitivity analysis re-computed the composite with D2 (author authority) and D6 (citation uptake) excluded and the remaining dimension weights renormalised. The high-credibility share (tiers A+B) rises from 24.7\% to 30.6\%, and the tier distribution shifts from A = 2, B = 279, C = 735, D = 118, E = 2 to A = 2, B = 346, C = 596, D = 176, E = 16. The structural inversion persists under this specification: governance remains the most credible subdomain (33.3\% to 40.9\% tier A+B) and evaluation the least credible large strand, so the headline structural findings are robust to excluding the source-prestige dimensions, while the absolute credibility shares shift upward. The full sensitivity scores are in the supplementary material (`research/quality-sensitivity-nod2d6.csv`).
% ============================================================

Quality was assessed on eight weighted dimensions (D1--D8), with paper-type-adjusted weights: D1 venue/peer-review tier (0.10); D2 author authority (0.15); D3 formal-methods rigour (0.15--0.25); D4 empirical reproducibility (0.20, empirical studies only); D5 argumentative rigour (0.15--0.25); D6 citation/field uptake (0.15); D7 transparency (0.05); D8 prior-literature engagement (0.05--0.15). Composite scores map to tiers: A (3.2--4.0), B (2.4--3.19), C (1.6--2.39), D (0.8--1.59), E (0.0--0.79). The source-based dimensions are near-orthogonal in practice---the D1--D2 correlation is $-0.17$, D1--D6 is $-0.03$, and D2--D6 is 0.12---so author authority and citation uptake are not redundant with venue tier. D6 applies a time-discounted citation baseline: recent work is compared against a shorter accumulation window (with the rubric's ``zero citations after 12 months'' floor), reducing the temporal bias toward older literature. Tier distribution across the 1,136 included studies: A = 2, B = 279, C = 735, D = 118, E = 2; median composite 2.15 (IQR 1.90--2.35). The full rubric, per-dimension weights, and per-study scores are in the supplementary material (`research/quality-criteria.md`, `research/quality-scores.csv`). Quality was used as a credibility signal only; no study was excluded on quality grounds.
